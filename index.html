Article · Forensic Science

The Science of Seeing: Why Eyewitness Testimony is Under Fire

In the legal system, few pieces of evidence are as powerful as a confident eyewitness pointing to a suspect and saying, "That's the person who did it." For decades, juries have viewed these testimonies as undeniable proof of guilt. However, recent developments in forensic science and cognitive psychology have revealed a troubling truth: the human brain does not function like a high-definition security camera.

When an innocent bystander to a high-stress event watches a crime unfold, their body goes into a natural "fight-or-flight" response. Adrenaline floods the system, causing tunnel vision. Under intense fear, an individual's attention is naturally drawn to the immediate threat—such as a vehicle swerving dangerously through an intersection—rather than the subtle physical characteristics or stature of the perpetrator.

Furthermore, human memory is highly malleable, meaning it can easily be altered after the fact. Psychologists have found that memory degrades rapidly over time and can be contaminated by "post-event information." For example, if a witness is repeatedly asked leading questions by investigators before giving their official statement, or if they discuss the event with others, their brain may unconsciously weave those new details into their original memory.

This creates a dangerous phenomenon where a witness becomes completely certain about details that are factually incorrect. DNA evidence has since exonerated hundreds of wrongfully convicted individuals, proving that eyewitness error is the single greatest cause of wrongful convictions nationwide. While human memories remain an essential starting point for police investigations, law enforcement now relies much more heavily on objective physical evidence, such as digital camera footage collected from local storefronts, or physical debris left at the scene, to verify testimonies before bringing a case to trial.
